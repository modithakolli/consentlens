# ConsentLens interaction principles

## The decision comes first

Show one calm, reversible answer before technical evidence: **pause and review**, **use caution**, or **no urgent concern**. Do not imply that a score is a legal, security, or moral verdict.

## Reveal evidence progressively

The default popup contains only the answer, up to three reasons, and three next actions. Policy clauses, vendor lists, historic events, graph nodes, and data-rights tools live behind named disclosures. A graph is shown only when it explains a relationship that text cannot: person → site → provider → recipient category.

## Make uncertainty specific

Use these distinct states:

- **Not observed**: the current scan did not see a signal.
- **Not analysed**: a linked policy or account setting was not checked.
- **Unclassified**: a network request was observed but is not yet mapped.
- **Source-backed**: an intelligence record has a current source and review date.

Never turn an absence of evidence into a safety claim.

## Preserve agency

Use direct actions such as “Review cookie choices”, “See what this means”, and “Draft a data request”. Do not use urgency, shame, absolute safety language, simulated certainty, or generic AI phrasing. Backend analysis must remain an explicit user action and say what will be sent before it runs.

## Accessibility and trust

Use plain language, predictable labels, keyboard-operable disclosures, visible focus, text alongside colour, and source links next to factual claims. Colour indicates attention level only; it is never the only carrier of meaning.

## Research basis

- Mathur et al., *Dark Patterns at Scale* (ACM CSCW, 2019): deceptive interface patterns can steer people into unintended choices.
- Nouwens et al., *Dark Patterns after the GDPR* (CHI, 2020): consent interfaces frequently make refusal materially harder than acceptance.
- Reidenberg et al., *Disclosures in Privacy Policies: Does Notice and Consent Work?* (2015): long, complex notices produce poor comprehension.
